12The passive

Form

be (in the right tense) + past participle (V3). The object of the active sentence becomes the subject.

Active: Shakespeare wrote Hamlet. → Passive: Hamlet was written by Shakespeare.

present perfecthas/have been + V3The homework has been done.
past perfecthad been + V3The room had been cleaned.
present continuousam/is/are being + V3The road is being repaired.
past continuouswas/were being + V3I was being watched.
willwill be + V3The results will be announced on Friday.

infinitiveto be + V3She wants to be invited.

When do we use the passive?

  • The doer is unknown or unimportant: My bike was stolen.
  • The action or result is more important than the doer: The bridge was built in 1998.
  • Formal / scientific writing: The samples were heated to 80 °C.
  • Use by + agent only when the doer is important or new information: Hamlet was written by Shakespeare.

Two objects

Verbs with two objects (give, send, show, tell, offer) have two passives: She gave me a book. → I was given a book. / A book was given to me.

Passive with reporting verbs

It is said that he is rich. = He is said to be rich. Also: believed, thought, known, expected, reported, claimed.

Have something done

When someone else does a service for you: have + object + V3. I had my hair cut. She is having her car repaired. (not I cut my hair, unless you did it yourself.)

Common mistakes

The accident was happened yesterday. → The accident happened yesterday. (happen, occur, arrive, die, fall, seem, exist have no passive)

The book writes by him. → The book was written by him.

It was cost 10 euros. → It cost 10 euros.

The letter was sended. → The letter was sent. (V3 of irregular verbs)

I am agree. → I agree.

13Conditionals and wishes

A conditional sentence has an if-clause (condition) and a main clause (result). If the if-clause comes first, put a comma. If it rains, we stay home. / We stay home if it rains.

TypeIf-clauseMain clauseMeaning · example
Zeropresent simplepresent simplealways true, facts: If you heat ice, it melts.
Firstpresent simplewill + V1real, possible future: If I study, I will pass.
Secondpast simplewould + V1unreal / unlikely now or future: If I had a million, I would travel.
Thirdpast perfectwould have + V3unreal past, too late: If I had studied, I would have passed.
Mixedpast perfectwould + V1past cause, present result: If I had slept, I would be fine now.
Details that matter

• In the second conditional use were for all persons in formal English: If I were you, I would apologise.
• Never put will or would in the if-clause: If I had time (not if I would have time).
• unless = if … not: Unless you hurry, you'll be late. (= If you don't hurry)
• Other modals are possible in the main clause: If you work hard, you can / may / should succeed.
• Inversion (formal): Had I known, I would have come. · Should you need help, call me. · Were I rich, I would retire.
• as long as, provided (that), on condition that mean "only if": You can go provided you finish your work.

14Reported speech

We report what someone said without quoting the exact words. If the reporting verb is in the past (said, told, asked), the tense usually moves one step back.

Direct speechReported speech
present simple: "I like pizza."She said (that) she liked pizza.
present continuous: "I'm working."He said he was working.
past simple: "I saw him."She said she had seen him.
present perfect: "I've finished."He said he had finished.
will: "I will call."She said she would call.
can: "I can swim."He said he could swim.
may: "I may go."She said she might go.
must: "I must go."He said he had to go.

No back-shift is needed when the reporting verb is present (She says she likes pizza) or when the statement is still true (He said the Earth is round). should, would, could, might do not change.

Questions, commands, requests

  • Yes/no questions: if / whether + statement order. "Do you like tea?" → She asked if I liked tea.
  • Wh-questions: wh-word + statement order (no do/does/did). "Where do you live?" → He asked where I lived.
  • Commands: told + person + to-infinitive. "Sit down!" → She told me to sit down. Negative: told me not to.
  • Requests: asked + person + to-infinitive. "Please help me." → He asked me to help him.

15.2 Purpose, adjectives, prepositions

  • Purpose: to + base. I went to the shop to buy milk. (not for buy, not for buying) Negative: in order not to.
  • Adjective + to-inf: happy to help, easy to learn, ready to go.
  • After a preposition always -ing: interested in learning, good at swimming, thank you for helping, before leaving, instead of going.
  • to + -ing after some phrases (to is a preposition): look forward to seeing, be used to working, object to paying.
  • Subject: an -ing form can be a subject: Swimming is fun.
used to / be used to / get used to

used to + V1 = past habit (ch. 6): I used to smoke.
be/get used to + -ing = be/become accustomed: I am used to getting up early. I'm getting used to living here.

15.3 Phrasal verbs

A phrasal verb = verb + particle (up, out, off, in, on, away, back, down). The meaning is often different from the verb itself. Learn them as whole units.

Separable (object can go in the middle): turn off the light / turn the light off / turn it off. With a pronoun the pronoun must go in the middle: turn it off, turn off it.

Inseparable: look after the baby, look after her (not look her after).
