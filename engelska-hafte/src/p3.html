16.2 Countable and uncountable nouns

Countable

can be counted: a book, two books. Use a/an, numbers, many, few, a few, fewer.

Uncountable

cannot be counted; no plural, no a/an. Use much, little, a little, less, or "a piece of / a bit of".

Common uncountable nouns: information, advice, news, furniture, luggage, equipment, homework, money, music, traffic, weather, knowledge, progress, research, work, travel, evidence, bread, water, rice, hair, health, help, permission. They take a singular verb: The news is good. My hair is long.

some is used in positive sentences and offers/requests (Would you like some tea?). any in negatives and most questions. every / each / all: every + singular (every student is); all + plural (all students are); each = one by one.

16.4 Possessive 's

  • singular: the girl's bag · plural ending in -s: the girls' bags · irregular plural: the children's toys.
  • Names ending in s: James's book or James' book (both correct; be consistent).
  • For things use of: the end of the film (not the film's end), the door of the car.
  • its (possessive) ≠ it's (it is / it has).

16.5 Subject-verb agreement

  • Singular subject → singular verb: The list of items is long. (the subject is list)
  • each, every, everyone, everybody, nobody, something, either, neither + singular verb: Everyone is ready.
  • Two subjects with and → plural: Tom and Ann are here.
  • either … or / neither … nor / not only … but also: the verb agrees with the nearer subject: Neither the teacher nor the students were there.
  • Names of subjects and illnesses with -s are singular: Mathematics is hard. Physics is fun. The news is bad.
  • Always plural: people, police, children, clothes, trousers, glasses, scissors: The police are here.
  • Money, distance, time as a unit: Ten dollars is a lot. Five years is a long time.
Common mistakes

There are many informations. → There is a lot of information.

Less people came. → Fewer people came.

Everyone are happy. → Everyone is happy.

My friend's are nice. → My friends are nice. (plural has no apostrophe)

The people is angry. → The people are angry.

More rules

  • the + adjective = group of people: the poor, the rich, the young, the elderly.
  • the + musical instruments: play the guitar; sports: no article: play football.
  • Decades: in the 1990s. Dates: on the 5th of May.
  • Names: no article (Mr Smith, Sweden, Stockholm, Lake Mälaren, Mount Everest), but the Netherlands, the Philippines, the Atlantic, the Thames.
  • "In general" → no article; "specific" → the. I love music. but The music at the party was loud.
  • Once, twice a day: twice a week, 60 km an hour.

  • Subject pronoun = does the action; object pronoun = receives it or follows a preposition: She gave it to me. After between, with, for, to always object: between you and me.
  • Possessive adjective before a noun: my book. Possessive pronoun stands alone: The book is mine.
  • Reflexive: when subject and object are the same: I cut myself. Or for emphasis: I did it myself. No reflexive after wash, shave, dress, feel, relax, concentrate if meaning is clear: I washed. She feels tired.
  • each other / one another: They help each other. (not themselves)
  • Indefinite: someone, anyone, no one, everyone, something, anything, nothing, everything. Use they/their for unknown gender: Someone left their bag.
  • Demonstratives: this/these (near), that/those (far): this book here, those books over there.
  • Double negative is wrong in standard English: I don't know nothing. → I don't know anything / I know nothing.
Common mistakes

Me and my friend went home. → My friend and I went home.

He gave it to she. → He gave it to her.

The book is her's. / It's own colour. → The book is hers. / Its own colour. (no apostrophe in possessives)

They love themselves. (meaning each other) → They love each other.

He took his bike and she took hers bike. → … and she took hers.

19Adjectives and adverbs

19.1 Adjectives

  • Adjectives have no plural form: two tall men (not talls). They stand before the noun or after be, seem, feel, look, become.
  • Order before a noun: opinion – size – age – shape – colour – origin – material – purpose + noun. a beautiful small old round red Italian leather bag. In practice use two or three adjectives at most.
  • -ed / -ing: I am bored (feeling) / The film is boring (cause). Also: interested/interesting, excited/exciting, tired/tiring, surprised/surprising.
  • Compound adjectives with numbers use singular: a ten-year-old girl, a two-hour film.

19.3 Adverbs

Adverbs describe verbs, adjectives, other adverbs or whole sentences. Many are formed with -ly: quick → quickly, careful → carefully, happy → happily, possible → possibly, true → truly.

AdjectiveAdverbNote
goodwellShe is a good singer. She sings well.
fast, hard, late, earlysame wordHe works hard. (hardly = almost not) · She arrived late. (lately = recently)
friendly, lovely, lonely, elderly(no -ly adverb)use "in a friendly way"

After sense verbs (look, feel, sound, taste, smell, seem, become) use an adjective: You look tired. It sounds good. The soup tastes great.

Intensifiers: very, really, extremely, quite, rather, too (negative: more than needed), enough (after adjective: old enough). too ≠ very: It's too cold to swim.

Common mistakes

more taller / most happiest → taller / happiest (never both)

He runs very quick. → He runs very quickly.

She speaks English good. → She speaks English well.

I feel me bad. → I feel bad.

He is more tall than me. → He is taller than me.

I am very agree. / I'm very interesting in sport. → I agree. / I'm very interested in sport.
