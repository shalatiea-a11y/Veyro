The 5 comma rules that cover 90 %

1) Lists: A, B and C (Oxford comma before "and" is optional but be consistent).
2) Introductory element: After school, I went home.
3) Two full clauses + and/but/so/or: comma before the conjunction. I wanted to go, but it rained.
4) Extra information (non-defining): commas on both sides.
5) Direct address / yes / no / however: Yes, I agree. John, come here.

No comma before "that" and no comma between subject and verb

I think, that he is right. → I think that he is right.   The boy, ran home. → The boy ran home.

Capital letters

Capitals for: first word of a sentence, I, names of people and places, nationalities and languages (Swedish, English, Arabic), days and months (Monday, May), titles before names (Dr Smith), main words in titles. No capitals for seasons, school subjects (except languages), compass directions in general (north), and a job title not with a name (the prime minister).

Sentence boundaries

Run-on sentence and comma splice

I like English it is fun. (run-on)   I like English, it is fun. (comma splice)

I like English. It is fun. · I like English because it is fun. · I like English; it is fun.

Because I was tired. (fragment) → I stayed home because I was tired.

28Writing a good text

28.1 Structure of an essay

Introduction

• Hook (question, fact, short story)
• Background
• Thesis: your main idea in one sentence

Body (2–3 paragraphs)

• One idea per paragraph
• Topic sentence
• Explanation, example, link back to the thesis

Conclusion

• Restate the thesis in new words
• Sum up your main points
• Final thought (no new arguments)

28.2 PEEL for a paragraph

PPointThe main idea of the paragraph
EEvidencefact, quote or example
EExplainwhy this supports your point
LLinkconnect back to the question / thesis

28.3 Style

  • Vary sentence length. Mix short sentences with longer ones that use linkers.
  • Be specific. A lot of people → about 60 % of teenagers. Prefer exact verbs: say → claim, explain, argue.
  • Avoid "thing", "stuff", "good", "bad", "very". Use beneficial, harmful, extremely useful.
  • Formal writing: no contractions (do not), no slang, no "you" for general statements (people / one), no exclamation marks.
  • Informal writing (blog, email to friend): contractions and short phrases are fine.
  • Be concise. Delete words that add nothing: in my personal opinion → in my opinion.
  • Active voice is usually stronger than passive.

28.4 Useful phrases

Starting
It is often said that …
Nowadays, …
Many people believe that …
This essay will discuss …
Arguing
One reason is that …
For example, …
This shows that …
Some people disagree because …
Ending
In conclusion, …
To sum up, …
Taking everything into account, …
For these reasons, …

28.5 Formal letters and emails

  • Dear Mr Smith, (name known) · Dear Sir or Madam, (name unknown)
  • Opening: I am writing to enquire about / to apply for / to complain about …
  • Closing: Yours sincerely (name known) · Yours faithfully (name unknown) · Kind regards (email)

29Proofreading: how to find your own mistakes

The 6-pass method

Read your text 6 times, each time looking for one kind of mistake. This works much better than reading once and looking for everything.

PassLook forCheck
1 Verbssubject-verb agreement, tense, irregular formsDoes every verb have a subject? he/she/it + -s? Same time frame? V2 vs V3?
2 Sentencesfragments, run-ons, comma splices, word orderDoes every sentence have subject + verb? Is it a complete idea?
3 Nounsarticles, plurals, uncountablesa / the / nothing? information (not informations)?
4 Small wordsprepositions, pronouns, linkersdepend on, good at, although … but?
5 Punctuationcommas, apostrophes, capitalsits/it's? their/there? capital for I, days, languages?
6 Spellingcommonly misspelled and confusing wordsRead backwards, word by word. Use a dictionary for doubtful words.
Proofreading tricks

• Wait (at least some hours) before you proofread.
• Read aloud slowly; your ear catches missing words.
• Change the look: print it or change the font.
• Know your own top 5 mistakes and search for those specifically.
• Read from the last sentence to the first to focus on the sentence, not the story.
