Connecting ideas

21Linking words

Linking words (connectors) show the relation between ideas. They make your writing flow and are an important part of a high grade.

FunctionLinkersExample
addingand, also, in addition, moreover, furthermore, besides, as well as, not only … but alsoThe course is cheap. Moreover, it is useful.
contrastbut, however, although, though, even though, despite, in spite of, whereas, while, on the other hand, nevertheless, yetI was tired, but I kept going. Although it rained, we went out.
reasonbecause, as, since, because of, due to, owing toWe stayed in because of the rain.
resultso, therefore, as a result, consequently, thus, that is whyIt rained. As a result, the match was cancelled.
purposeto, in order to, so that, so as toI study so that I can get into university.
conditionif, unless, as long as, provided that, otherwiseHurry up, otherwise we'll be late.
time / orderfirst, then, next, after that, finally, meanwhile, while, as soon as, eventuallyFirst mix the flour. Then add eggs.
examplefor example, for instance, such as, like, in particularMany fruits, such as apples, are healthy.
summaryin conclusion, to sum up, overall, in short, all in allIn conclusion, exercise is important.
opinionin my opinion, I believe, personally, it seems to meIn my opinion, school should start later.

Grammar of the contrast words

WordFollowed byExample
although / though / even thoughsubject + verbAlthough he was ill, he went to work.
despite / in spite ofnoun or -ingDespite the rain / Despite being ill, he went.
however, neverthelessstarts a new sentence; comma afterHe was ill. However, he went to work.
but, yetjoins two clauses (no comma needed unless long)He was ill but he went to work.
while, whereascompares two thingsHe loves tea, whereas she prefers coffee.
because / because of · so / such

because + clause (because it rained) · because of + noun (because of the rain).
so + adjective/adverb (so cold) · such + (a/an) + adjective + noun (such a cold day). so many / so much.

Common mistakes

Although it was cold, but we went out. → Although it was cold, we went out. (not both)

Despite of the rain … → Despite the rain … / In spite of the rain …

I was late, however I wasn't sorry. (comma splice) → I was late; however, I wasn't sorry. / I was late. However, I wasn't sorry.

Because I was tired. I went to bed. → Because I was tired, I went to bed.

It was so a good film. → It was such a good film.

22Relative clauses

A relative clause gives more information about a noun. It starts with a relative pronoun.

PronounRefers toExample
whopeople (subject / object)The woman who lives next door is a doctor.
whichthings, animalsThe book which I read was great.
thatpeople or things (defining clauses only)The man that called is my uncle.
whosepossessionThe girl whose bag was stolen cried.
whereplacesThis is the school where I studied.
whentimesI remember the day when we met.
whyreasonsThat is the reason why I left.
whomformal object of peopleThe person whom I met was kind.

Defining and non-defining

Defining (necessary)

Tells which one. No commas. that possible. Pronoun can be left out if it is the object.
The film (that) we saw was long.

Non-defining (extra info)

Extra information. Commas around it. No that. Pronoun cannot be left out.
My brother, who lives in Umeå, is a nurse.

  • Whole-sentence which: He passed the test, which surprised everyone.
  • Preposition: The house in which I live (formal) = The house (that) I live in. (normal)
  • Reduced clauses: the girl sitting there (= who is sitting), the book written by him (= which was written)
  • Do not repeat the subject/object: The man who he called …
Common mistakes

The man which called … → The man who / that called …

The book that I read it was good. → The book that I read was good.

My mother, that is a teacher, … → My mother, who is a teacher, …

The girl which bag was stolen … → The girl whose bag was stolen …

What I like is the thing that is cheap what … → All that I need is … (what = "the thing that"; do not combine)

23Question tags and short answers

23.1 Question tags

A question tag turns a statement into a question asking for agreement. Positive statement → negative tag. Negative statement → positive tag. Use the same auxiliary as in the statement (or do/does/did if there is none).

StatementTag
You are tired,aren't you?
She likes tea,doesn't she?
They didn't come,did they?
He can swim,can't he?
You've finished,haven't you?
I am late,aren't I? (special)
Let's go,shall we? (special)
Open the door,will you? (imperative)
Nobody called,did they? (nobody/nothing = negative)

Falling intonation = you are sure; rising intonation = you are really asking.

23.2 Short answers and echo questions

Answer yes/no questions with the auxiliary: Do you like tea? – Yes, I do. / No, I don't. · Have you finished? – Yes, I have. · Can you swim? – No, I can't. · Are you ready? – Yes, I am.

  • So / neither: agreeing. I like pizza. – So do I. · I don't like fish. – Neither do I. / Nor do I. The auxiliary matches the tense: I've seen it. – So have I.
  • Echo questions: I'm tired. – Are you? shows interest.

Common mistakes

You like tea, isn't it? → You like tea, don't you? (not the Arabic/Indian-English "isn't it?")

Who did call you? → Who called you?

What you want? → What do you want?

She doesn't like it, doesn't she? → She doesn't like it, does she?
